# Isolating Regulation — the Identification Strategy

This is the methodological heart of your dissertation and the thing that will most impress your supervisor. You said it precisely:

> "make sure the difference I am measuring is not changed due to the change of technology or what people like, but due to regulations and laws that make them do this kind of design in a redevelopment project."

In research-methods language, you want to **attribute the change in publicness to the regulatory/legal regime (the *treatment*) while controlling for three confounds**:

- **C1 — Technology** (construction methods, materials, CCTV/access tech, step-free access tech).
- **C2 — Preferences / taste** (what residents, markets and architects "like" in a given era).
- **C3 — Site/context** (location, transport access, surrounding land uses).

A naïve "1951 vs 2018" comparison cannot separate these — that is exactly the weakness you spotted. Below is a four-part strategy that does. Use all four together; each closes a different loophole.

---

## Strategy A — Measure only *regulation-sensitive* attributes (design the confound out)

The single most powerful move is **what you choose to measure**. Publicness is largely a product of *rules, ownership and management* — i.e. regulation — **not** of technology or taste. So by scoring publicness rather than "quality" or "attractiveness", you have already half-solved the problem.

Split every candidate attribute into two lists and **only score the left column**:

| ✅ Regulation/governance-driven (SCORE THESE) | ❌ Technology/taste-driven (DO NOT score) |
|---|---|
| Ownership & management regime (public / POPS / estate-managed) | Architectural style (Brutalist / glass / neo-traditional) |
| Legally secured public access (24h right vs revocable licence) | Cladding material, structural system |
| Posted rules / conduct codes / prohibited activities | Whether it "looks nice" |
| Security & policing regime (public vs private guards) | Fashionable amenities (e.g. coffee kiosks) |
| % affordable housing & tenure secured by S106 | Smart-tech / app-based features |
| Provision secured by obligation (toilets, play space, seating) | Soft-landscaping species choice |
| Permeability/access points *required* by policy | Step-free access *enabled by modern lifts* (tech) |

**Defence you can write:** "Because the dependent variable (publicness) is operationalised through ownership, access rights, rules and management — attributes determined by the regulatory regime rather than by construction technology or aesthetic preference — variation in the measure is, by construction, weakly exposed to technological and taste-based confounding."

> Caveat to handle honestly: a few items sit on the border (e.g. defensive/"hostile" design is *enabled* by materials but *chosen* by management). Flag these and treat them as governance choices, justified in the text.

## Strategy B — Same-site "failed-and-rebuilt" pairs (holds C3 = site constant)

Your own idea, and a strong one. Where a space **failed fast, was demolished, and rebuilt on the same ground**, location/context is held constant by design. The before→after publicness difference is then attributable to the *regime that governed each build* (welfarist public provision vs market-led/viability redevelopment).

Verified Thames-side pairs:

| Pair | "Old" (failed) | "New" (rebuilt) | What is held constant | What changed (treatment) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **P1 Robin Hood Gardens → Blackwall Reach** | GLC Brutalist estate, completed **1972**, Poplar (by Blackwall/Docklands); demolished 2017–2025 | **Blackwall Reach** (Swan/Met Workshop & Haworth Tompkins); ~1,575 homes, 45–60% affordable | Same site, same riverside East London context | GLC public welfare provision → market-led mixed-tenure regeneration |
| **P2 Heygate Estate → Elephant Park** | GLC/Southwark estate, **1974**, Elephant & Castle; demolished 2011–2014 | **Elephant Park** (Lendlease); marketed off-plan overseas; ~private-led | Same site, same inner-South-London context | Public ownership → private master-developer + viability |
| **P3 Thamesmead (stages 1–2)** | GLC riverside "town of the future", **1966–74**; partial, failed | **Peabody regeneration** (2014–) | Same riverside SE-London site | GLC public new-town → housing-association-led regeneration |

These pairs are the backbone of your "old vs new on the same ground" comparison.

> **But B alone does NOT control C1/C2** — the new build is also newer-tech and newer-taste. That is what Strategy C fixes.

---

## Strategy C — Contemporaneous matched pairs (holds C1 *and* C2 constant) — THE KEY CONTROL

This is the move that genuinely isolates regulation. **Compare two spaces built in the *same period* (so same construction technology and same prevailing taste) but under *different regulatory regimes*.** Any publicness difference between them cannot be explained by tech or taste, because those are held constant by the matched dates — it must come from the regime.

Concrete contemporaneous contrasts available on/near the Thames:

| Same era (tech & taste held constant) | Regime X (less regulated / market-led) | Regime Y (more regulated / publicly governed) |
|---|---|---|
| **1980s** | LDDC / Enterprise Zone space in Docklands (planning relaxed) | A contemporaneous space delivered under **normal borough planning control** elsewhere in London |
| **2010s** | A **POPS** consented under viability (e.g. More London-type estate-managed plaza; One Blackfriars) | A 2010s space delivered as **adopted public highway/park** under full obligation |
| **Around 2020** | A POPS consented **just before** the Public London Charter / London Plan D7 | A comparable space consented **just after** the Charter applied |

**Defence you can write:** "By pairing schemes of the same vintage, technological capability and architectural idiom are held approximately constant; the residual difference in publicness is therefore attributable to the regulatory regime under which each was consented."

---

## Strategy D — Regulatory discontinuities (the explanatory timeline)

Pin each shift in publicness to a **specific, datable change in law/policy**, so your explanation names the regulation rather than gesturing at "neoliberalism". These are the "treatments" on your timeline:

| Year | Regulatory change | Expected effect on publicness |
|---|---|---|
| **1980** | Local Government, Planning and Land Act → Urban Development Corporations & Enterprise Zones (planning relaxed) | ↓ (enclaved, privately managed) |
| **1990s–2000s** | PPG3 / Urban Task Force (1999) → density + design codes; CABE | mixed |
| **2012** | NPPF + the "viability" turn (public benefits become the residual) | ↓ (affordable & public realm squeezed) |
| **2017→2021** | London Plan POPS policy (D7) + **Public London Charter (2020)** | ↑ (access rights re-asserted) |

Plotting publicness against this timeline lets you say *which law did what* — and tests Clifford's thesis (deregulation degrades outcomes) directly, plus whether **re-regulation (the Charter) reverses it**.

---

## How the four combine (one paragraph for your methods chapter)

> The longitudinal trajectory (five phases) provides the descriptive backbone. Causal attribution to regulation is secured by (A) restricting measurement to regulation-sensitive attributes of publicness; (B) same-site failed-and-rebuilt pairs that hold location constant; (C) contemporaneous matched pairs that hold technology and taste constant; and (D) mapping each inflection to a datable regulatory discontinuity. Triangulating B and C means no single confound can account for the observed pattern: B rules out site effects, C rules out technology and taste, A minimises their entry in the first place, and D names the operative regulation.

This is a recognised **small-N comparative / natural-experiment logic** (most-similar-systems design + before–after pairs). State plainly that it supports *analytical generalisation* (theory-testing), not statistical generalisation.
