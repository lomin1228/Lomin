## 1. The question

> **How do three contemporary waterfront regenerations — built in the same broad period on post-industrial harbourfront land — structure affordable housing differently, on what rationale, and which model best balances market stability with durable affordability?**

This is **not** a design-philosophy comparison (well-trodden). It is **not** "what is the right land tax rate?" (not researchable). It is a **comparative study of housing delivery architecture** — who builds affordable homes, through what tenure instruments, funded by what cross-subsidy, and justified by what account of each city's conditions.

The sustainability test you set is precise and researchable:

> *Which rationale delivers affordable housing without breaking the housing market — i.e. without suppressing supply, distorting land prices beyond recovery, or producing affordability labels that do not hold at delivery?*

---

## 2. Why this is a genuine niche

| Literature | What it studies | What it ignores |
|---|---|---|
| **Waterfront regeneration** (Gordon, 1997; Boland and Muir, 2017) | Public access, design, governance | **Tenure structure** — how affordable housing is actually built |
| **Affordable housing semantics** (Scanlon, 2017; Preece et al., 2020; your Path A) | Label drift in England | Cross-national **delivery mechanism** comparison |
| **Value capture** (Crook et al., 2016; Foye, 2022) | S106, CIL, betterment | Waterfront-specific tenure design; HK/NYC instruments |

**The gap:** Comparative waterfront studies discuss *design principles* or *publicness*; affordable housing studies discuss *English tenure labels* — but nobody compares **how three global cities structure affordable housing delivery on contemporary waterfront sites facing the same macro conditions** (post-industrial land, high locational rent, housing shortage, 2010s–2020s delivery).

## 4. Research question and sub-questions

**RQ:** *How do Kai Tak, Hunter's Point South and Thamesmead Waterfront structure affordable housing delivery in their contemporary regeneration plans — and which city's rationale most sustainably balances market functioning with durable affordability?*

**SQ1.** What is the **tenure mix** specified for each case (public/social rent; subsidised ownership; affordable rent; market rate) and who delivers it (state, HA, private developer)?

**SQ2.** What **rationale** does each city advance for its chosen housing structure — and how does that rationale reflect local conditions (land ownership, housing crisis type, governance capacity)?

**SQ3.** What are the **documented strengths and constraints** of each model for delivering affordable housing on high-value waterfront land?

**SQ4.** Against a sustainability framework (delivery certainty, tenure depth, market signal preservation, fiscal durability), **which model best balances affordability with market stability** — and under what conditions?

## 6. Housing structure analysis — what to compare in each city

### 6.1 Hong Kong — Kai Tak

**Structure:**
- **Public Rental Housing (PRH)** — direct government build on public land (Sites 2B2–2B6; ~3,120 flats per cluster)
- **Subsidised Sale Flats (SSF) / Green Form (GSH)** — URA and HA sell at discount to market (e.g. URA Kai Tak: 338 SSF units at 20% below market; 2016)
- **Private residential** — sold by land premium at auction; developer builds market-rate flats
- **Flexibility clause:** housing type can shift between PRH/GSH/SSF subject to demand (Planning Brief, Sites 2B3/2B4)

**Rationale (from documents):**
- Acute housing shortage; Kai Tak is "optimisation of scarce land resources" (TPB, A/K22/35)
- 2018: nine private housing sites reallocated to public housing — government can override market allocation when supply crisis demands
- Waterfront premium land used for both prestige (private towers) and social obligation (PRH clusters)
- Executive-led government: rezoning commercial → residential (+460,000 m² GFA, +9,000 flats) without market negotiation

**Pros:**
- High **delivery certainty** on public sites — government builds directly, no viability test
- **Tenure depth** — PRH serves lowest income; not a label but a physical product
- Can **reallocate land use** when market fails to deliver (2018 private → public switch)

**Cons:**
- **Market distortion** — land premium on private sites may not reflect true scarcity if public sites are pre-allocated
- **Middle-income gap** — SSF at HK$3.4–6.6m still excludes many; "affordable" is relative to market, not income need
- **Fiscal burden** — direct build on premium harbourfront is expensive; sustainability depends on land revenue from private sites cross-subsidising
- **Waterfront publicness** — PRH towers on harbourfront may deliver housing but not the "public creativeness" promenade identity Kai Tak UDGM promises elsewhere

---

### 6.2 New York — Hunter's Point South

**Structure:**
- **Site A (city-owned):** 5,000 units — **60% permanently affordable** (3,000 units), 40% market rate
- **Site B (private):** 1,650 units — **20% affordable** (330 units) via inclusionary zoning
- **Income bands:** 40%–165% Area Median Income (AMI) — low, moderate, middle, and "workforce" tiers
- **Permanent covenants:** affordability locked in perpetuity, not time-limited
- **Phase 1 exceeded target:** 75% affordable (685 of 908 units) via competitive RFP (Bloomberg, 2011)

**Rationale (from documents):**
- "Largest affordable housing development since the 1970s" — explicit crisis response (NYCEDC; HPD)
- **Publicly owned waterfront land** as the enabling condition — city does not need to negotiate with landowners
- Part of city-wide 165,000 affordable unit target (EIS Final Scope, 2008)
- Park-first strategy: 11-acre waterfront park (2018) as amenity enabling housing density and market acceptance

**Pros:**
- **Scale** — 3,000+ permanently affordable units on prime waterfront is exceptional by global standards
- **Permanent covenants** — affordability does not expire at year 30 (unlike many US LIHTC schemes)
- **Public land leverage** — avoids residual land value negotiation entirely on Site A
- **Mixed-income** — avoids pure ghettoisation; 40% market rate maintains social mix and market signal

**Cons:**
- **Lottery access** — allocation by lottery, not need-ranked; lowest-income households face long odds
- **AMI band problem** — units at 130–165% AMI serve upper-middle income, not the poorest; "affordable" label covers wide range
- **Site B weakness** — only 20% affordable on private land; two-tier system within same project
- **Market dependency** — 40% market rate on Site A needed to cross-subsidise; if market softens, whole model is at risk
- **Single-project anomaly** — depends on city owning the land; not replicable on privately owned waterfront without different instruments

---

### 6.3 London — Thamesmead Waterfront

**Structure:**
- **Peabody–Lendlease JV** (50/50) on HA-owned land
- **35% minimum affordable** (Greenwich policy); **40% aspiration** (New Towns Taskforce / MHCLG 2026)
- **Tenure mix (expected):** social rent, affordable rent (≤80% market), shared ownership — per Peabody's standard delivery model and Lesnes Estate precedent (35% on 1,950 units, 2021)
- **Mechanism:** Section 106 negotiation + viability assessment; Peabody as long-term steward
- **Unlock condition:** DLR extension (~£1.5bn) — transport infrastructure as precondition for viability

**Rationale (from documents):**
- "Isolated and underutilised riverside location" — brownfield, not prime central London (Peabody, 2019)
- HA stewardship: Peabody owns 65% of Thamesmead; mission-driven, not pure market
- New towns programme: 40% affordable, half social rent — but Peabody CEO: "viability will be the test of delivery" (Inside Housing, 2025)
- Lesnes Estate precedent: reprovision-only "unviable"; 35% declared maximum deliverable (Peabody Planning Statement, 2021)

**Pros:**
- **Market-compatible** — viability model preserves developer profit; less likely to suppress private supply
- **HA stewardship** — Peabody holds land long-term; not a one-off disposal
- **Tenure range** — can include genuine social rent, not just intermediate "affordable rent"
- **Whole-place regeneration** — links to existing community (45,000 residents), not greenfield luxury

**Cons:**
- **Viability erosion** — Lesnes Estate already shows 35% as ceiling under viability test; 40% aspiration may not bind at waterfront phase
- **Delivery uncertainty** — no outline consent yet for Waterfront; commitments are masterplan-level, not consent-level
- **"Affordable" semantic drift** — your Path A finding applies directly: label may not mean social rent at delivery
- **Transport dependency** — without DLR, viability fails; affordability contingent on public infrastructure spend
- **S106 durability** — negotiated, deferrable, amendable; Heygate/Elephant Park precedent shows erosion over time

## 7. Sustainability framework — how to judge "most sustainable"

Define **sustainable** explicitly in the dissertation — not as "best for everyone" but as:

> *A housing delivery rationale is sustainable when it (a) produces affordable units at or near the promised rate, (b) maintains tenure depth for those who need it most, (c) does not suppress market supply or produce fiscal conditions that make the model self-undermining, and (d) locks in affordability beyond a single development cycle.*

| Criterion | What to measure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **C1 Delivery certainty** | Are units built/on track? Is the % binding in consent or aspirational? | Planning consents, build data, document commitments |
| **C2 Tenure depth** | Does "affordable" include lowest-income households, or only intermediate bands? | Tenure schedules, income targeting, AMI/band definitions |
| **C3 Market compatibility** | Does the model require market rate cross-subsidy? What happens if market softens? | Viability docs, fiscal structure, % market rate required |
| **C4 Fiscal durability** | Can the model fund itself beyond initial subsidy? | Land premium, PILOT, S106, government capital |
| **C5 Affordability lock-in** | Are covenants permanent or renegotiable? | Legal instruments, S106 schedules, covenant terms |

**Predicted ranking (state as hypothesis, then test):**

| Criterion | Kai Tak (HK) | Hunter's Point South (NYC) | Thamesmead (London) |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 Delivery certainty | **High** — public build direct | **High** — Phase 1 delivered | **Low** — pre-consent; viability-qualified |
| C2 Tenure depth | **High** for PRH; weak for SSF | **Moderate** — wide AMI bands; lottery | **Moderate** — social rent possible but viability-constrained |
| C3 Market compatibility | **Low** — state overrides market | **Moderate** — 40% market needed | **High** — viability preserves market signal |
| C4 Fiscal durability | **Moderate** — land premium cross-subsidy | **Moderate** — public land is one-off asset | **Low–moderate** — S106 + HA subsidy |
| C5 Lock-in | **High** — PRH permanent stock | **High** — permanent covenants | **Low** — S106 renegotiable |

**Expected conclusion (honest, not polemical):**

No single city "wins" on all criteria. The dissertation should argue:

- **Hunter's Point South** is most sustainable on **lock-in and scale** (permanent covenants, 60% on public land) — but only works where the city **owns the waterfront land**, which is rare.
- **Kai Tak** is most sustainable on **delivery certainty and tenure depth** for the poorest — but **distorts market signals** on premium land and depends on fiscal cross-subsidy from private land sales that may not persist.
- **Thamesmead** is most sustainable on **market compatibility** — but **least reliable on delivery and tenure depth** because viability and S106 allow affordability to erode between masterplan and consent (your Path A finding applied to a new site).

**The planning insight:** The "most sustainable" model depends on **land ownership at the outset**. Public land enables NYC/HK-style direct delivery; private/HA land in a residual viability system (London) produces market compatibility at the cost of affordability certainty. This is the original contribution.

Adapt the RCF from `18/00` for housing structure analysis. Score each document 0–3:

| # | Dimension | What to code |
|---|---|---|
| H1 | **Tenure types specified** — social rent / PRH; affordable rent; subsidised sale; market | Structure |
| H2 | **Delivery agent** — state / HA / private / JV | Governance |
| H3 | **Affordable % binding** — in consent, statute, or aspiration | Certainty |
| H4 | **Income targeting** — lowest income vs intermediate vs AMI bands | Depth |
| H5 | **Cross-subsidy mechanism** — land premium / public land / S106 / viability / lottery | Finance |
| H6 | **Rationale for city's condition** — housing crisis type, land ownership, governance | Context |
| H7 | **Market compatibility** — % market rate required; viability language | Sustainability |
