## A. Theoretical frame — variegated neoliberalisation (the periodisation you "agree on")

### Peck, J. and Tickell, A. (2002) 'Neoliberalizing space', *Antipode*, 34(3), pp. 380–404.
- **What it says:** The foundational roll-back / roll-out distinction. *Roll-back* = 1980s dismantling of Keynesian-welfare institutions and deregulation; *roll-out* = active state-building of new market-facilitating institutions (BIDs, partnerships, regeneration vehicles, design codes).
- **Why include:** This is your *periodisation engine*. Each waterfront site can be located on this timeline: Docklands/LDDC = roll-back; Royal Docks OAPF + Mayoral development corporations = roll-out.
- **Best use:** Theory + structuring the "phases" of your comparison. **Critique to make:** it is largely Anglo-American — which is exactly why bringing in Hong Kong's *state-leasehold* model stress-tests the theory (your original contribution).

### Brenner, N., Peck, J. and Theodore, N. (2010) 'Variegated neoliberalization: geographies, modalities, pathways', *Global Networks*, 10(2), pp. 182–222.
- **What it says:** Neoliberalisation is not one thing rolled out identically; it is *variegated* — path-dependent, interacting with inherited institutions, producing different local forms.
- **Why include:** This is the single concept that *justifies a three-city comparison*. London, NYC and HK are three "pathways" of the same process. It answers the examiner's "so what / why compare?" question.
- **Best use:** Your headline theoretical contribution. **Operationalise it** (your notes: "find one concept, make it operational, measurable") by treating publicness scores as the *measurable trace* of variegation.

### Harvey, D. (1989) 'From managerialism to entrepreneurialism: the transformation in urban governance in late capitalism', *Geografiska Annaler B*, 71(1), pp. 3–17.
- **What it says:** Cities shift from *managing* services to *entrepreneurially competing* for capital via spectacle and flagship development.
- **Why include:** Waterfront flagship regeneration (Hudson Yards' Vessel, Kai Tak's promenade, Royal Docks) is the textbook case of entrepreneurial urbanism. Your notes cite Harvey ("class project") — this is the precise paper to cite.
- **Best use:** Explains *why* spectacular but exclusionary public space gets built.

## B. The object of study — publicness & public space (your "one element")

### Németh, J. and Schmidt, S. (2011) 'The privatization of public space: modeling and measuring publicness', *Environment and Planning B: Planning and Design*, 38(1), pp. 5–23. doi: 10.1068/b36057.
- **What it says:** Builds an **observation-based index** to *measure* how controlled a space is across dimensions (laws/rules, surveillance/policing, design/image, access/territoriality). Finds privately-owned public spaces (POPS) use more behaviour-controlling features even when access is nominally open. Studied NYC.
- **Why include — THIS IS YOUR QUANTITATIVE CORE.** It converts your NYC "Ritz-Carlton POPS" observation (open 24h but designed to repel) into a *number you can compare across three cities*. Easy to interpret, replicable, defensible.
- **Best use:** Methodology + data collection instrument. See `03 methodology.md` for the adapted coding sheet.

### Varna, G. and Tiesdell, S. (2010) 'Assessing the publicness of public space: the star model of publicness', *Journal of Urban Design*, 15(4), pp. 575–598. ⚠ *(volume/issue strongly indicated but confirm before citing pages)*
- **What it says:** A complementary model scoring five meta-dimensions — ownership, control, civility, physical configuration, animation — producing a **"publicness star" (radar chart)**.
- **Why include:** Gives you a second, visual, **easy-to-read radar chart per site**. Triangulating Németh & Schmidt (control-focused) with Varna & Tiesdell (broader, includes positive "civility/animation") protects you from the critique that you only measured *restriction*.
- **Best use:** The figure your examiner remembers — three overlaid radar stars, one per city.

### Carmona, M. (2015) 'Re-theorising contemporary public space: a new narrative and a new normative', *Journal of Urbanism*, 8(4), pp. 373–405. doi: 10.1080/17549175.2014.909518.
- **What it says:** **Your supervisor's colleague at the Bartlett.** Argues the public-space literature is too uniformly pessimistic ("end of public space"). Based on mixed-methods study of *new and regenerated London public spaces*, he offers a *positive, pragmatic "new normative"* — public space is plural, contingent, and often works.
- **Why include:** (1) Direct Bartlett anchor; (2) the perfect *counter-thesis* to Minton. Your dissertation can adjudicate between Minton's pessimism and Carmona's optimism **using your own index data**. That is a genuine debate to resolve = distinction territory.
- **Best use:** Framing + the critical debate your findings arbitrate. Also see Carmona & Wunderlich (2012) *Capital Spaces* (Routledge) for the London typology.

### Minton, A. (2009/2012) *Ground Control: Fear and Happiness in the Twenty-First-Century City*. London: Penguin.
- **What it says:** Polemic on the privatisation of British public space (BIDs, gated/"managed" estates, the Olympic Park) eroding citizenship and trust.
- **Why include:** The accessible, influential *pessimist* pole. Strong on Docklands/Olympic legacy → directly relevant to Royal Docks.
- **Best use / critique:** Use as a *position to test*, not as evidence — it is journalistic/polemical (your notes: "media for discourse not understanding"). Pair it with the scholarly POPS literature.

### Kohn, M. (2004) *Brave New Neighborhoods: The Privatization of Public Space*. New York: Routledge. ⚠ *(confirm exact pages when quoting)*
- **What it says:** Political-theory account of why privatised public space matters for democracy, free assembly and inclusion.
- **Best use:** The *normative "why should we care"* — useful in your introduction and conclusion to lift the stakes beyond design.

## Synthesis — how these combine into a distinction-level argument

1. **Frame:** Variegated neoliberalisation (Brenner/Peck/Theodore) says the *same* global pressure produces *different* local public-space outcomes.
2. **Debate:** Minton (pessimist) vs Carmona (pragmatist) disagree on whether regenerated public space is genuinely public.
3. **Method:** Németh & Schmidt + Varna & Tiesdell let you *measure* publicness and *adjudicate* that debate.
4. **Supervisor anchor:** Clifford's "deregulation degrades quality" thesis is your testable hypothesis, transposed from housing to public space and made comparative (Madeddu & Clifford).
5. **Contribution:** You produce comparable publicness scores across three governance models, showing *which institutional pathway protects publicness best* — empirically resolving the Minton/Carmona debate with new data.
