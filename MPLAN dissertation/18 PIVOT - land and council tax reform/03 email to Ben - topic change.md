**Subject:** BPLN0008 — possible change of dissertation topic (land and property tax reform)

Dear Ben,

I hope the summer is going well.

I am writing because I would like to ask whether it is possible to change my dissertation topic, and if so what the process is. I submitted the Thamesmead / Elephant Park proposal on 30 June as planned, and I am happy to continue with it if changing is not sensible at this stage — I am not asking you to rescue anything, and I have not started collecting the new material.

The topic I would like to move to is **the reform of land and property taxation in Britain**, studied the same way we agreed in June: published documents only, a pre-specified coding instrument, no interviews, no model of my own.

The specific question is not "how should land be taxed", which I know is not researchable. It came out of reading your work on permitted development, and it is narrower:

> When planning control over commercial use was removed, did anything replace it — and is the tax system now doing that job by accident?

In September 2020 Use Class E removed local authorities' ability to control what occupies a shop unit, so a bookshop can become a vape shop or a nail bar with no application at all. In April 2026 the business rates system acquired more or less that power, through permanently lower retail, hospitality and leisure multipliers funded by a high-value multiplier. The instrument that shapes high street composition appears to have moved from planning to tax, designed in a different department on purely fiscal and sectoral criteria, and I cannot find anyone who has examined the transfer as a land-use decision.

The wider literature supports treating this as a planning question rather than a fiscal one. Brueckner and Kim (2003) and Banzhaf and Lavery (2010) show the choice of tax base changes urban form, and Lockwood, Simmler and Tam have just published causal estimates in the *AEJ: Economic Policy* showing that UK business rate reliefs substantially change commercial occupancy and vacancy — including that empty property exemption increases vacancy by up to 89%. Meanwhile, where planning does still have an instrument, the takeaway management zone evaluations show it works. So the instruments matter; the question is which system holds them and on what evidence.

I would study it as two strands of the same reform moment, coded the same way:

- **Non-domestic** — the 2026 revaluation and multiplier package, business rate reliefs, High Street Rental Auctions, set against the 2015 impact assessment that made betting shops *sui generis* and the 2020 one that created Class E
- **Domestic** — the High Value Council Tax Surcharge, announced at Budget 2025, with a consultation that ran 19 May to 14 July 2026 and responses now emerging, compared with Wales (which studied a land value tax and legislated for five-yearly revaluation from 2028 instead), Scotland (where the Land Commission declined to recommend one) and Northern Ireland as a short control case

Each document would be coded on how deeply it engages with seven rationales, running from revenue adequacy through to land use behaviour and interaction with the planning system — the same instrument logic as the ASI in the June proposal. The expected finding is that the fiscal rationales are everywhere and the spatial ones are largely absent, which would be a governance point about the split between the Treasury and MHCLG rather than an argument for or against any particular tax.

Three things I am unsure about and would value your view on:

1. Whether a topic change is permitted at all now that the proposal has been marked, and whether I need to speak to the module lead as well as you.
2. Whether this sits close enough to your interests to supervise. It reads to me as the next question in the deregulation work you did with Jessica Ferm, Nicola Livingstone and Patrícia Canelas, but you are much better placed to judge that than I am, and I did not want to go around you by asking anyone else first.
3. Whether the live-policy risk worries you. Burnham has spoken about council tax reform for years but nothing formal has been announced. My plan would be a hard evidence cut-off of 31 January 2027, with anything later treated as a coda, on the basis of what you said in June about researching what has happened.

If you would rather I stayed with Thamesmead, that is genuinely fine and I will carry on with the document harvest as planned.

Thank you,

James (Yick Long) Li
MPlan City Planning
