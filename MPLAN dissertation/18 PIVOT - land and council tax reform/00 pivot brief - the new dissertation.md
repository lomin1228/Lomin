## 1. The one thing to fix first

Your stated goal — *"understand how to tax land appropriately in the best way… without it being too high or too low"* — is a **normative optimisation question**, and it is not researchable in 15,000 words. Worse, it is precisely the thing Ben ruled out in June: *"speculative policy invention without empirical base"* and *"the best dissertation researches something that has happened or is happening and you collect data about that."*

You cannot derive the correct rate of land tax. Nobody can — that is the whole point of the 117-year British argument.

But the motive behind your question is excellent, and it converts cleanly. Here is the conversion:

> **Not:** what is the right way to tax land?
> **But:** when governments actually design land and property tax reform, *what do they reason about, and what do they leave out?*

That is answerable, it is document-based, it is non-ideological by construction, and — critically — the answer turns out to be a **planning** answer, not a public-finance one. That is what makes it a Bartlett dissertation rather than an IFS working paper.

## 2. The gap (this is the dissertation)

Two literatures exist and they do not talk to each other.

**Literature A — public finance.** Council tax is regressive, based on 1991 values, and needs revaluation (Mirrlees *et al.*, 2011; Adam *et al.*, 2020). A land value tax is efficient because land is in fixed supply, so the tax cannot be shifted (George, 1879; Mirrlees *et al.*, 2011). These arguments are about **revenue, incidence and distribution between households**.

**Literature B — urban economics and planning.** The choice of tax base changes *the shape of cities*. Taxing improvements as well as land depresses the capital/land ratio and can encourage sprawl (Brueckner and Kim, 2003). Split-rate taxation in Pennsylvania measurably raised housing density, and did so through *more units* rather than bigger units (Oates and Schwab, 1997; Banzhaf and Lavery, 2010; Yang, 2014). Under the Henry George theorem, aggregate land rents at optimal city size exactly fund pure public goods — so the "right" level of land taxation is a **spatial** proposition, not just a fiscal one (Arnott and Stiglitz, 1979).

**The gap:** British land tax reform is argued almost entirely in Literature A's terms. Open the High Value Council Tax Surcharge consultation (HM Treasury/MHCLG, 2026) and you find valuation methodology, banding, appeals, protected characteristics, ability to pay. You will find close to nothing about density, vacancy, land supply, development timing, urban form, or the interaction with Section 106 and CIL. **The tax that shapes land is being designed without reference to land use.**

That absence is your finding, and it is measurable across documents.

**The sharpest instance of it is the high street**, and this is what makes the project unmistakably a planning dissertation rather than a fiscal one. In September 2020, Use Class E removed the planning system's power to control what occupies a shop unit. In April 2026, the business rates system acquired that power through differential multipliers by use and rateable value. The instrument that shapes high street composition moved from planning to tax, was designed by a different department on entirely fiscal criteria, and nobody examined the transfer as a land-use decision. **Read `04 the high street case` — that strand should carry roughly half the empirical weight.**

## 4. Research question and sub-questions

**RQ:** *In British land and property tax reform documents (2018–2026), to what extent — and how — is the reform justified in spatial and land-use terms rather than purely fiscal and distributional ones, and what explains the variation between jurisdictions?*

**SQ1.** What rationales are advanced in England's High Value Council Tax Surcharge documents (Budget 2025 → consultation May–July 2026 → responses), and how are spatial effects treated?

**SQ2.** How do Wales (Bangor 2019 technical assessment; Local Government Finance (Wales) Act 2024; Welsh Government, 2026) and Scotland (Scottish Land Commission, 2018; 2022) differ — both examined LVT seriously and both chose incrementalism instead. On what grounds?

**SQ3.** How do the stated grounds for rejecting or deferring LVT compare with the reasons the academic literature gives (valuation feasibility, discretionary planning incompatibility, transition politics) — Hughes *et al.* (2020); Morgan and Shahab (2023)?

**SQ4.** What does the historical record of failed British betterment taxation (1947, 1967, 1976, 2007) suggest about which design features determine durability — and are those features present in the 2026 reforms?

**SQ5 (the high street strand).** Following the removal of planning control over commercial use mix by Use Class E in 2020, on what basis does the 2026 business rates multiplier package differentiate between uses — and does that basis draw on any land-use evidence?

## 5. The analytical instrument

You already built an ASI (Affordability Semantics Index) for the old proposal. Build the same kind of thing here. It is the single biggest distinction marker — examiners can verify a pre-registered coding rubric.

**Rationale Coding Frame (RCF).** Score each document 0–3 on the *depth* of engagement with each of seven rationales (0 = absent, 1 = mentioned, 2 = discussed, 3 = drives a design decision):

| # | Rationale | Type |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | Revenue adequacy and stability | Fiscal |
| R2 | Distribution between households (regressivity, ability to pay) | Fiscal |
| R3 | Distribution between **places** (North/South, urban/rural, LA funding) | Spatial-fiscal |
| R4 | **Land use behaviour** — vacancy, under-use, density, development timing | Spatial |
| R5 | **Interaction with the planning system** — S106/CIL, land supply, allocations, hope value | Spatial |
| R6 | Valuation and administration — data, cadastre, revaluation cadence | Technical |
| R7 | Transition and political durability — phasing, caps, deferral, lock-in | Design |

**Predicted result (state it, then test it):** R1, R2, R6 score high everywhere. R3 is high in England (levelling-up framing) and in the IFS work. **R4 and R5 collapse to 0–1 in the English documents but score 2–3 in the Welsh and Scottish ones.** If that holds, the argument is that devolved administrations, which own planning *and* local tax together, reason spatially — while England, where the Treasury owns the tax and MHCLG owns planning, does not. That is a **governance** finding, which is squarely Ben's territory.

## 7. The Burnham question — handle with tongs

**The facts as of today.** Andy Burnham became Prime Minister in July 2026 (MP for Makerfield), with John Healey at the Treasury replacing Rachel Reeves. He has backed a land value tax since his 2010 leadership bid, repeatedly calls council tax "highly regressive", and has said there is "a big case for land and property and business taxation to be changed". Two models are reportedly in play: the Fairer Share **proportional property tax** (0.48% of current value, replacing council tax *and* stamp duty) and an **LVT**. **No formal plan has been adopted.** Number 10 has said property tax is a matter for fiscal events.

**Rules for using this.**

1. **He is the "why now", not the object of study.** Anchor your introduction in the live reform moment; do not build a chapter on a policy that does not exist yet.
2. **Provenance rule for discourse data.** Admit only on-record sources: Hansard, official transcripts, published speeches, government publications. Exclude "sources tell *The i Paper*". Write this rule into your methodology — it is a distinction-level move, because it shows you know the difference between journalism and evidence.
3. **Fairer Share and Property Tax Lab are advocacy/grey literature.** Cite them as *objects of analysis* (they are participants in the debate) never as authority for a factual claim.
4. **Volatility mitigation.** Set a hard **evidence cut-off date** (suggest 31 January 2027). Anything announced later becomes a short coda in the conclusion. Ben already blessed this logic in June: *"you research what happened."* Your design is about reasoning *in documents*, so a new announcement simply becomes one more document rather than invalidating the study.

**The genuinely interesting Burnham angle** is not the PPT-versus-LVT parlour game. It is this: Greater Manchester under Burnham spent a decade building the most advanced **land-value-capture-adjacent fiscal machinery** in England — 100% business rates retention from 2017, extended for ten years under the 2023 Trailblazer deal, plus Growth Zones and Investment Zones with 25-year retention and no reset. That is a mayor who governed by capturing *locational* value through *business* property tax while having no lever at all over *domestic* property tax. If he now reforms council tax nationally, the question is whether the spatial reasoning that shaped GM's business rates deals transfers into the domestic reform — or whether the Treasury's fiscal frame reasserts itself. **That is a testable proposition and it is the best thing in this brief.**

## 8. The "not too high, not too low" problem, properly named

Your instinct has a real academic lineage. Do not abandon it — reframe it as the **calibration problem**, and note that all three failure modes are documented:

- **Too low or badly based:** the tax is capitalised into land prices and does nothing; land is held idle; sprawl is subsidised (Brueckner and Kim, 2003; Ryan-Collins, Lloyd and Macfarlane, 2017).
- **Too high or badly transitioned:** owners face unmanageable step changes and the market locks up. Denmark's 2024 reform had to bolt on a permanent, non-transferable discount for existing owners precisely to prevent this — solving the transition problem but creating a lock-in problem instead.
- **Too fast:** every British national betterment tax was repealed within a decade — the 1947 development charge (repealed 1953), the 1967 Land Commission betterment levy (repealed 1970), the 1976 Development Land Tax (repealed 1985), and the Planning-gain Supplement (abandoned 2007 before enactment). The ACT in Australia responded by choosing a **20-year** transition, and evaluations after eight and ten years still struggle to isolate the effect.

So the honest scholarly answer to "what is the right level?" is: **a land tax is appropriately set when it is (a) high enough to make withholding land costly, (b) transitioned slowly enough to survive politically, and (c) revalued often enough to stay legible.** Britain has never achieved (b) and (c) simultaneously. Council tax has now gone 35 years without revaluation in England, while the durable mechanism — Section 106 — works precisely because it is negotiated, local and largely invisible (Crook, Henneberry and Whitehead, 2016; Foye, 2022).

**That is your conceptual framework, and it is not ideological.** It does not say land should be taxed more or less. It says reforms fail on design and durability grounds, and asks whether the 2026 reforms have learned.
