# 22 — The high street case: use classes, reliefs, and unwanted uses

**This is the file that makes the dissertation a planning dissertation.** Everything in `00 pivot brief` still stands, but this strand should carry roughly half the empirical weight, because it is where land taxation and land-use control physically collide — on a shopfront.

It is also the strand that puts you closest to Ben. He is a co-author of the leading academic critique of exactly this deregulation.

---

## 1. The paradox in one paragraph

In **September 2020**, the new **Use Class E** collapsed shops, offices, restaurants, cafés, health premises, nurseries, gyms and professional services into a single planning class. A bookshop can now become a vape shop, a barber, a nail bar, a gym or an estate agent with **no planning application, no consultation, and no council decision**. One planning law commentator called it "one of the most significant rollings back of regulatory control in the planning system for decades", and it was done by amending secondary legislation.

In **April 2026**, the business rates system acquired precisely the power that planning had lost. New **permanently lower retail, hospitality and leisure multipliers**, set 5p below the national equivalents for properties under £500,000 rateable value, funded by a **high-value multiplier** 2.8p above the standard rate for properties at £500,000 and above.

> **England has quietly transferred the instrument that shapes high street composition from the planning system to the tax system — and nobody has examined it as a land-use decision.**

Planning lost the lever in 2020. Tax gained one in 2026. The Treasury designed the new one on fiscal and sectoral criteria; MHCLG owns the planning question; the two documents do not cite each other. That is your R4/R5 gap, made concrete on a street you can walk down.

## 2. Your "wanted and unwanted shops" instinct — where the line actually falls

The existing English settlement is genuinely arbitrary, and the arbitrariness is documentable:

| Use | Planning status | Tax treatment (2026–27) | Evidence on whether control works |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Betting shops, payday loan shops, pawnbrokers** | *Sui generis* since 16 Apr 2015 — permission always required | Standard/RHL multiplier | **Largely ineffective.** Jones *et al.* concluded the 2015 change did little to control clustering; refusal invites expensive appeals |
| **Hot food takeaways** | *Sui generis* — permission required | RHL multiplier (hospitality) | **Works.** Management zones around schools cut new outlets by around 81% at six years and raised refusal rates by 18.8 percentage points, rising to 46.1 points for full zones |
| **Vape shops, barbers, nail bars** | **Class E — no control whatsoever** | **Lower RHL multiplier, as "retail"** | No instrument exists to evaluate |
| **Independent bookshop, greengrocer** | Class E | Lower RHL multiplier | Same treatment as a vape shop |
| **Vacant unit** | n/a | Empty property relief — **increases vacancy by up to 89%** | High Street Rental Auctions (Dec 2024) introduced to patch it |

Read that table twice. **A vape shop and a bookshop are the same thing in both the planning system and the tax system.** A betting shop needs planning permission but pays the same multiplier. A hot food takeaway that planning would refuse next to a school gets hospitality relief if it is already trading. And a landlord who lets a shop stand empty is rewarded by the tax system until a council goes through a 24-week statutory auction process to stop them.

Nobody designed this. It accreted. Documenting the incoherence, rigorously and from primary sources, is a genuine contribution.

## 3. The finding that will make your examiner sit up

Here is the crucial thing about your "tax relief for shops for prosperity" idea, and you need to confront it head-on rather than propose it.

**Business rates reliefs work — and then they leak to the landowner.**

The causal evidence is strong and very recent. Lockwood, Simmler and Tam (2026), published in the *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy* this month, exploit discontinuities in UK business rate reliefs and find that **retail relief and small business rate relief both substantially reduce commercial vacancies**, that small business relief sorts the occupier mix towards small businesses and away from large ones, and that **empty property exemption increases vacancies by up to 89%**. They also find both reliefs are **more effective in less deprived areas** — so a uniform national relief is spatially regressive, doing least where high streets are weakest.

But a parallel literature finds that in the medium term reliefs are **capitalised into rents**, so the benefit accrues to the property owner rather than the occupier (see the discussion in Matikonis's evaluation work, and the older incidence literature). Welsh survey evidence found 12% of tenants reported landlords taking most of the relief in extra rent, while two-thirds reported no increase — short-run evidence, but the direction is clear.

**So the honest conclusion is Georgist, and you arrive at it empirically rather than ideologically:**

> A relief attached to *occupation* of a site eventually flows into the *rent* of that site. You cannot durably subsidise good shops through a tax on occupation, because the subsidy is a property of the location, and locations are owned. If you want the relief to reach the shopkeeper rather than the landlord, the tax base has to be the land, not the occupancy.

That is a complete, evidence-backed answer to your original question — "how do you make land prosperous without taxing it too high or too low" — delivered on a specific case, with causal estimates, without a word of advocacy. It is also the single best paragraph in the dissertation and you should build towards it.

## 4. Empty property relief and rental auctions: a tax distortion patched by regulation

Follow this sequence, because it is the cleanest story in the whole project:

1. **Empty property relief** exempts vacant commercial property from rates for a period. Lockwood, Simmler and Tam estimate it raises vacancy by up to 89%. The tax system pays owners to keep shops empty.
2. Vacant units hollow out town centres — the thing every high street policy since the Portas Review has tried to reverse.
3. Rather than reform the relief, government legislated a **regulatory** fix: **High Street Rental Auctions**, Part 10 of the Levelling-up and Regeneration Act 2023, in force 2 December 2024. Councils can designate high street areas and, where premises have been vacant for 365 continuous days (or 366 days within 24 months), compel the landlord to let via auction on a one-to-five-year lease, with a permitted development right attached so the new use does not need permission.
4. Early evidence suggests the **threat** of auction often produces voluntary letting: Broxtowe served England's first notice in September 2025 and the owner refurbished and let the unit rather than proceed.

**The analytical point:** a distortion created by *tax* design was addressed by a *planning and property* instrument, twelve years later, on a 24-week statutory process, running through local authorities who must fund the designation and survey. That is what a system without R4/R5 reasoning looks like from the inside. It is also a perfect illustration for your conclusion.

## 5. Where this sits in the dissertation

Restructure the empirical work into two strands, coded on the **same** R1–R7 frame:

**Strand A — domestic.** How we tax where people live. Council tax, the 1991 valuation base, the High Value Council Tax Surcharge (2025 Budget, consultation May–July 2026), Welsh revaluation from 2028, the Scottish and Northern Irish comparators.

**Strand B — non-domestic.** How we tax where people shop. The 2026 revaluation and multiplier package, RHL and high-value multipliers, small business and empty property reliefs, High Street Rental Auctions, and the Class E deregulation that removed the planning alternative.

Strand B is where R4 (land use behaviour) and R5 (planning interaction) *should* score highest, because the policy is explicitly about high streets. If it still scores low — if the RHL multiplier documents talk about sectors and rateable values but never about town centre role, clustering, frontage, or the Use Class Order — then your argument is made twice over, in the domain where the excuse of irrelevance is unavailable.

**Suggested revised title:**

> **"Taxing the high street, planning the high street: the separation of fiscal and land-use reasoning in English property tax reform, 2020–2026"**

Or keep the broader title from `00` and make this Chapter 4b. Discuss with Ben.

## 9. Two things to be careful about

**First, do not let this become a shopping-list dissertation about vape shops.** The vape shop is an *illustration* of a structural point about which system holds the instrument. If Chapter 4 turns into a survey of nuisance retail, you lose the analytical spine. One paragraph on vape shops, doing hard work, is worth ten pages of examples.

**Second, watch for a live policy development.** In recent coverage of vape shop proliferation, a government spokesperson was quoted saying ministers are "giving local people new powers to block vape shops", alongside Pride in Place funding. **Verify this properly** — find the actual announcement, consultation or statutory instrument on gov.uk or in Hansard before relying on it. If a real Class E reform or a new use class is in train during 2026–27, it becomes a central document for you rather than a footnote. Check monthly, and apply the same provenance rule: a quotation in a news article is a lead, not a source.
