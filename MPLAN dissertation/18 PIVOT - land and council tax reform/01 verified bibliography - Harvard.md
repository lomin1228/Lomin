## A. Foundational theory — the case for taxing land

**1. George, H. (1879) *Progress and Poverty*. New York: Appleton.** ⚠️ *(canonical text; use a modern scholarly edition and cite that edition's details)*
- The origin of the argument that land value is created by the community and should therefore be taxed for the community. Also the origin of the claim that a land tax cannot be shifted to tenants because land supply is fixed.
- **Best use:** one paragraph in Chapter 2 to establish lineage. **Do not** build the dissertation on George. Every weak LVT dissertation over-invests here.

**2. Arnott, R. and Stiglitz, J.E. (1979) 'Aggregate land rents, expenditure on public goods, and optimal city size', *The Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 93(4), pp. 471–500. doi: 10.2307/1884466.** ✅
- The Henry George Theorem: at optimal city size, aggregate differential land rents exactly equal expenditure on pure public goods.
- **Why it matters to you:** this is the formal answer to your "how much is appropriate?" question — and it is fundamentally *spatial*, tying the right level of land taxation to city size and public goods provision. It is the single best citation for showing that land taxation is a planning question, not only a fiscal one.
- **Best use:** conceptual framework, Chapter 2. Cite the result; do not attempt the maths.

**3. Mirrlees, J. et al. (2011) 'The taxation of land and property', in *Tax by Design: The Mirrlees Review*. Oxford: Oxford University Press for the Institute for Fiscal Studies, ch. 16.** ✅
- Proposes replacing business rates with a land value tax starting around ½% of land value, and replacing council tax and stamp duty with a **Housing Services Tax** — a flat proportion of up-to-date property value, standing in for the VAT that housing consumption escapes. Calls stamp duty a tax with "little to say in its defence". Notes that Northern Ireland already runs a reformed domestic rates system on 2005 capital values (capped at £400,000).
- **Best use:** the benchmark against which you score every subsequent reform document. It is also the source of the crucial distinction between a **land** value tax and a **property/housing services** tax — a distinction the political debate constantly blurs and which you should police carefully.

## B. Urban form — how tax base choice changes cities

**4. Brueckner, J.K. and Kim, H.-A. (2003) 'Urban sprawl and the property tax', *International Tax and Public Finance*, 10(1), pp. 5–23. doi: 10.1023/A:1022260512147.** ✅
- Formal model: taxing improvements depresses the capital/land ratio, pushing cities outward; but a countervailing dwelling-size effect can dominate. Net effect on city size is **ambiguous**, and depends on the elasticity of substitution.
- **Best use:** this is your honesty anchor. It stops you claiming that a land tax straightforwardly densifies cities. Use it to show you understand the theory is indeterminate — which is exactly why the empirical work below matters.

**5. Oates, W.E. and Schwab, R.M. (1997) 'The impact of urban land taxation: the Pittsburgh experience', *National Tax Journal*, 50(1), pp. 1–21.** ✅
- The canonical empirical study of split-rate taxation, examining Pittsburgh's 1979 increase in the land rate. Found increased construction activity.

**6. Banzhaf, H.S. and Lavery, N. (2010) 'Can the land tax help curb urban sprawl? Evidence from growth patterns in Pennsylvania', *Journal of Urban Economics*, 67(2), pp. 169–179.** ✅
- Panel study of Pennsylvania municipalities. Confirms the split-rate tax raises the capital/land ratio, and shows the effect comes through **more housing units rather than bigger ones** — i.e. genuine density, not just larger houses. Estimates a 2–10 percentage point increase in housing-unit density growth in the first two decades.
- **Best use:** the strongest available evidence that tax base choice has real spatial consequences. This is the empirical fact that makes the absence of R4/R5 in British documents interesting rather than merely pedantic.

**7. Yang, Z. (2014) *The Effects of the Two-Rate Property Tax: What Can We Learn from the Pennsylvania Experience?* Working Paper WP14ZY1. Cambridge, MA: Lincoln Institute of Land Policy.** ✅
- Replicates and strengthens Banzhaf and Lavery with better data; finds larger effects.
- **Best use:** a footnote confirming the finding is robust. Do not spend words on it.

## C. Britain — council tax, its regressivity, and reform

**8. Adam, S., Hodge, L., Phillips, D. and Xu, X. (2020) *Revaluation and Reform: Bringing Council Tax in England into the 21st Century*. IFS Report R168. London: Institute for Fiscal Studies. doi: 10.1920/re.ifs.2020.0168.** ✅
- The definitive analysis. Council tax is "out of date, regressive and distortionary": Band H pays three times Band A despite being worth at least eight times as much in 1991. Revaluation plus proportionality would cut average bills by over 20% across most of the Midlands and the North, and raise them in London and its commuter belt — but **only if central government funding is reallocated in line with new valuations**. If not, average bills per authority are unchanged and the reform is redistributive within areas only.
- **Best use:** your primary quantitative reference point and your R3 (spatial-fiscal) exemplar. Note that even the IFS treats the spatial question as one of *fiscal geography*, not *land use* — which supports your gap.

**9. Crook, T., Henneberry, J. and Whitehead, C. (eds.) (2016) *Planning Gain: Providing Infrastructure and Affordable Housing*. Chichester: Wiley-Blackwell. doi: 10.1002/9781119075103.** ✅ *(RTPI research excellence award winner)*
- The authoritative account of how English planning obligations became, in the authors' words, "a successful *de facto* betterment tax" — succeeding where national *de jure* taxation repeatedly failed.
- **Best use:** the central book of your Chapter 2. It sets up the paradox at the heart of your dissertation: Britain's durable land value capture mechanism is the one nobody designed as a tax.

**10. Foye, C. (2022) 'Section 106, viability, and the depoliticization of English land value capture policy', *International Journal of Urban and Regional Research*, 46(2), pp. 269–286. doi: 10.1111/1468-2427.13076.** ✅
- Argues betterment value capture was depoliticised between the 1980s and 2008 across three "faces": economic interests (rising home ownership and rentierisation), institutions (viability governance handed to unelected experts and models, reducing LPA discretion), and discourse (Labour's convergence with Conservative framing).
- **Best use:** your explanatory mechanism in Chapter 5. If spatial reasoning is absent from tax reform documents, Foye tells you *why* — the debate was moved out of politics and into technical viability machinery. Also connects directly to your existing Clifford/viability reading, so it is not a wasted investment.

**11. Andrew, A., Pitt, M. and Tucker, M. (2007) 'The evolution of betterment in the United Kingdom', *Journal of Retail & Leisure Property*, 6(4). doi: 10.1057/palgrave.rlp.5100076.** ⚠️ *(page range not confirmed — check)*
- Traces the two competing strands in British betterment policy: central revenue collection versus local planning-based levy.
- **Best use:** a compact history citation so Chapter 2 does not become a narrative. Supplement with primary legislation for the actual dates.

**12. House of Commons Housing, Communities and Local Government Committee (2018) *Land Value Capture*. Tenth Report of Session 2017–19, HC 766. London: House of Commons.** ✅
- Concluded there is scope for government to claim a greater share of land value uplift; recommended reform of the Land Compensation Act 1961 to remove **hope value** from compulsory purchase compensation; recommended simplifying CIL; warned that any new mechanism needs cross-party support, simplicity, and no complicated viability processes if it is to survive.
- Pair with: **Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government (2018) *Land Value Capture: Government Response to the Select Committee Inquiry*. Cm 9734. London: HMSO.** ✅
- **Best use:** an excellent RCF coding unit — the inquiry reasons spatially, the government response largely does not. That contrast is a result in itself.

## D. Land value tax — implementation, feasibility and the planning interface

**13. Hughes, C., Sayce, S., Shepherd, E. and Wyatt, P. (2020) 'Implementing a land value tax: considerations on moving from theory to practice', *Land Use Policy*, 91, 104494. doi: 10.1016/j.landusepol.2020.104494.** ✅
- Examines six countries to ask why LVT is so rarely adopted despite the theoretical case. Of 167 countries surveyed by Almy, only seven tax land as distinct from land-plus-improvements. Identifies the binding constraints: a comprehensive up-to-date land registry, plot-level forward planning of land use, a resourced valuation profession able to separate land from improvements on a highest-and-best-use basis, and broad political support.
- Crucially notes that LVT is a **blunt instrument** for bringing forward under-used land: where land is unviable to develop it has no value to tax, and raising holding costs on housebuilders' pipelines may simply push them from land purchase into options.
- **Best use:** essential. This is the source that keeps your dissertation honest and stops it reading as advocacy. Also the intellectual bridge to your existing viability reading.

**14. Morgan, J.R. and Shahab, S. (2023) 'Impact of land value tax on the equity of planning outcomes', *Land*, 12(6), 1152. doi: 10.3390/land12061152.** ✅
- **Your nearest competitor — read it first.** Fifteen semi-structured interviews with tax and planning experts on LVT in Wales. Finding: eleven of fifteen interviewees saw taxation as having **no direct influence** on the planning system, with planners reporting "limited interaction" in day-to-day work. Council tax and NDR were seen to affect land use only indirectly, through exemptions for empty homes, second homes and single occupancy. Concludes LVT is **largely incompatible with a discretionary planning system** and that its benefits would be better achieved through reforms to existing taxes and land value capture mechanisms.
- **Best use:** two jobs. First, it is your gap: they interviewed people prospectively about Wales; you analyse the reform documents of four jurisdictions retrospectively, including an English reform that postdates their fieldwork. Second, their "tax and planning don't talk to each other" finding is exactly the phenomenon your RCF measures in documents rather than interviews. Their qualitative finding predicts your documentary one — that is a strong methodological triangulation argument.

**15. Welsh Government (2020) *A Technical Assessment of the Potential for a Local Land Value Tax in Wales*. Cardiff: Welsh Government.** ✅ *(commissioned from Bangor University; research conducted 2019, published March 2020 — check exact author attribution for the reference)*
- Found an LVT in Wales could in principle raise revenue comparable to council tax and NDR with more progressive distributive effects, but identified substantial implementation barriers. Explicitly covers **links to the planning system** as one of its terms of reference.

**16. Welsh Government (2026) *Testing Land Valuation Methods: A Land Value Tax for Wales? Claims and Contexts*. Cardiff: Welsh Government.** ✅ *(published February 2026)*
- The most recent official British assessment. Concludes valuation quality and revaluation frequency are decisive for realising intended outcomes; Wales does not currently operate a valuation system designed to isolate unimproved land value at scale; establishing one would require substantial investment in data infrastructure, professional skills and administrative capacity. Notes the Welsh Government's own test that alternatives must be shown to be "demonstrably better … not just different".
- **Best use:** a headline primary document. Its explicit attention to planning competencies is precisely the R5 engagement you predict is missing in England.

**17. Scottish Land Commission (2018) *Land Value Tax: Policy Options for Scotland*. Inverness: Scottish Land Commission.** ✅ *(research by the University of Reading)*
- Sets out four options ranging from reform of existing taxes to a single LVT replacing all land and property taxes.

**18. Scottish Land Commission (2022) *Land Reform and Taxation: Advice to Scottish Ministers*. Inverness: Scottish Land Commission.** ✅
- Explicitly **does not** propose a single land value tax. Recommends instead strengthening the role of land value in the tax base incrementally, beginning with bringing all land onto the valuation roll and developing a cadastral system. Frames tax around land reform objectives: diversifying ownership, town centre regeneration, just transition to net zero, supporting active farming.
- **Best use:** the clearest example of a British body reasoning about land tax in *land use* terms rather than fiscal ones. Very high predicted R4/R5 scores. This is the contrast case that makes England's silence visible.

## E. Land, rent and the political economy of British housing

**19. Ryan-Collins, J., Lloyd, T. and Macfarlane, L. (2017) *Rethinking the Economics of Land and Housing*. London: Zed Books.** ✅ *(open access via UCL Discovery)*
- Argues housing crises, financial instability and inequality are driven by land economy dynamics and the land-credit feedback cycle; proposes LVT and a national land bank.
- **UCL relevance:** Ryan-Collins is at the UCL Institute for Innovation and Public Purpose and has lectured at the Bartlett School of Planning. Worth an email; worth naming to Ben.
- **Best use:** theoretical framing for why land value matters macro-economically. Use sparingly and critically — it is the most normatively committed source on this list, and leaning on it will make your dissertation read as advocacy.

**20. Gallent, N. (2019) *Whose Housing Crisis? Assets and Homes in a Changing Economy*. Bristol: Policy Press.** ✅
- Housing's social purpose as home subordinated to its economic function as asset; asks what role planning might play in re-prioritising the social function.
- **UCL relevance:** Gallent is Head of the Bartlett School of Planning. Citing your own school's head is not sycophancy, it is context — and he is a plausible second reader.

**21. Haila, A. (2016) *Urban Land Rent: Singapore as a Property State*. Chichester: Wiley-Blackwell.** ✅
- Develops a theory of urban land rent through Singapore, where state landownership and 99-year leaseholds allow systematic public capture of development uplift through land premiums.
- **Best use:** two pages maximum, as the outer limit of the design space — the case where the land question was resolved through *tenure* rather than *taxation*. Do not let it become a chapter.

**22. Alterman, R. (2011) 'Land-use regulations and property values: the "windfalls capture" idea revisited', in Brooks, N., Donaghy, K. and Knaap, G.-J. (eds.) *The Oxford Handbook of Urban Economics and Planning*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, ch. 33, pp. 755–786. doi: 10.1093/oxfordhb/9780195380620.013.0034.** ✅
- Comparative survey of value capture across thirteen advanced economies; distinguishes macro, direct and indirect instruments; traces how the direct "windfalls capture" idea was incrementally transformed into a plethora of indirect instruments. Notes Britain coined the "betterment and compensation" vocabulary in the late nineteenth century and led the world with the 1909 Act.
- **Best use:** your comparative and definitional backbone. The macro/direct/indirect typology is a ready-made structure for Chapter 2 and pairs naturally with your RCF.

**23. Purcell, T.F. and Ward, C. (2022) 'The political economy of land value capture in the UK: rent and viability in Salford's new municipalist turn', *Environment and Planning A*. ⚠️ *(volume, issue and pages not confirmed — check via White Rose repository)*
- Land value capture and viability in a **Greater Manchester** authority pursuing municipalist delivery.
- **Best use:** the bridge between your Burnham interest and serious scholarship. If you want a Greater Manchester thread, this is where it belongs — not in newspaper coverage of No. 10.

## F. International design lessons — transition, not ideology

**24. ACT Treasury / NATSEM and TTPI (2020) *Analysis of the Impacts and Outcomes of the ACT Tax Reform*. Canberra: ACT Treasury.** ✅
**25. ACT Treasury / Centre of Policy Studies (2020) *The Economic and Efficiency Impacts of Altering Elements of the ACT's Tax Mix*. Canberra: ACT Treasury.** ✅
- Two independent evaluations of the Australian Capital Territory's 20-year programme, begun 2012, replacing conveyance stamp duty and insurance duty with broad-based land taxation through general rates, designed to be revenue-neutral. Both struggle to isolate the policy effect from wider market movements; estimates suggest modest price effects of up to around 2%, considered inconclusive.
- **Best use:** the empirical answer to "can you transition slowly?" — yes, but the evidence base after a decade is still thin. Excellent for a measured, non-ideological conclusion.

**26. Denmark, *Ejendomsskatteloven* (Property Tax Act), in force 1 January 2024.** ⚠️ *(cite the Act properly; supplement with Danmarks Nationalbank analysis)*
- Reassessed all properties on 2022 values, cut rates so that liabilities stayed broadly flat, moved *grundskyld* collection from municipalities to the state, and gave existing owners a **permanent, non-transferable discount** that resets on sale.
- **Best use:** the best contemporary worked example of solving the transition problem — and of the new problem it creates, since a discount that dies on sale is a lock-in incentive. This is the concrete content of "not too high, not too low".
- 🔶 **Caution:** a working paper titled 'The Lock-in Discount: Property Tax Reform and Housing Market Liquidity in Denmark' surfaced in searching, hosted at an unfamiliar venue. **Do not cite it.** Use Danmarks Nationalbank's published analysis and the Act itself instead, or find a peer-reviewed replacement.

## G. Primary documents — England 2025–26

**27. HM Treasury (2025) *Budget 2025*. London: HM Treasury.** ⚠️ *(26 November 2025; get the exact HC number)*
- Announced the High Value Council Tax Surcharge and the business rates multiplier package.

**28. Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, HM Treasury and HM Revenue & Customs (2026) *High Value Council Tax Surcharge*. Consultation, 19 May 2026 – 14 July 2026. London: MHCLG.** ✅
- **Your central primary document.** An annual charge on owners of English residential property worth £2m or more in 2026 prices, effective April 2028. Four bands: £2,500 (£2.0–2.5m), £3,500 (£2.5–3.5m), £5,000 (£3.5–5m), £7,500 (£5m+), uprated by CPI from 2029–30. Revaluations every five years from 2033. Fewer than 1% of English properties in scope. Estimated to raise around £430m a year; **revenue flows to central government, not to local authorities**, though local authorities administer and bill it. Consults on the definition of "owner", a support mechanism for those who cannot pay, discounts and exemptions, billing, challenge and enforcement, and equalities impacts.
- Note the government's own framing: the average Band D bill in England is £2,280, which is £250 *more* than a £10m Mayfair property pays under Band H in Westminster. That comparison is a fiscal-fairness argument with no spatial content whatsoever — a perfect RCF illustration.

**29. Office for Budget Responsibility (2026) *Supplementary Forecast Information Release: Costing of High Value Council Tax Surcharge*. London: OBR.** ✅
- Confirms revenues flow to central government; behavioural response reduces the static yield by around a third.
- **Best use:** the behavioural-response figure is your evidence that the Treasury *does* model behaviour — just financial behaviour, not locational behaviour. Sharp point.

**30. Local Government Finance (Wales) Act 2024 (asc 6).** ✅
- Establishes a five-yearly cycle of council tax revaluations in Wales from 1 April 2028 and three-yearly NDR revaluations; provides flexibility over band structure and the Band D reference point.

**31. Non-domestic rating: 2026 revaluation and multiplier package (England), effective 1 April 2026.** ✅
- Permanently lower retail, hospitality and leisure multipliers set 5p below national equivalents for properties under £500,000 rateable value, funded by a high-value multiplier 2.8p above the standard multiplier for properties at £500,000 and above. 2026–27 multipliers: small business RHL 38.2p, small business 43.2p, standard RHL 43.0p, standard 48.0p, high-value 50.8p.
- **Best use:** England has just introduced *differential rates by property type and value* in the non-domestic system. That is a live precedent for differentiation in the domestic system, and nobody seems to have connected the two. Good discussion material.

**32. Greater Manchester Combined Authority and HM Government (2023) *Greater Manchester Combined Authority Trailblazer Deeper Devolution Deal*. London: HM Government.** ✅
- 100% business rates retention for ten years from 2024/25; Growth Zones and Investment Zones with 25-year retention and no reset.
- **Best use:** the Burnham thread, done properly — through fiscal-devolution documents rather than newspapers.
