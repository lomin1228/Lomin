# London Longitudinal Design — comparing regeneration across time

**Confirmed direction:** London-only, **comparative across historical phases** of regeneration (not across cities). This satisfies the brief's "longitudinal study" requirement and lets the *neoliberalisation periodisation* (which your supervisor told you to "agree on") do real analytical work — time *is* the comparison axis.

## The research question

> **How has the *publicness* of public space produced through London's riverside regeneration changed across successive phases of urban policy from the post-war era to the present — and what explains the trajectory?**

### Objectives
1. **Periodise** London regeneration policy into defensible phases (documentary/historical).
2. **Select one comparable public-space exemplar per phase** along a single riverside transect (holds site-type and geography roughly constant, so change maps onto *policy/delivery model* over time).
3. **Measure** the publicness of each exemplar with one consistent instrument (your delivery hierarchy + Németh & Schmidt + Varna & Tiesdell + GIS).
4. **Explain** the trajectory through neoliberalisation theory + the viability mechanism.
5. **Adjudicate** the *privatisation* (Minton) vs *public-isation* (Carmona) debate with the longitudinal evidence; assess the **Public London Charter (2020)** as a corrective.

> You don't have to use all five — a strong dissertation could take **Phases 1–4** (1980s → today), or even three sharply contrasted exemplars (Docklands vs More London vs One Blackfriars). Fewer, deeper cases score better than many shallow ones.

### The recommended fieldwork transect (feasibility win)
A single **walkable Thames South Bank / Bankside transect (Lambeth–Southwark)** literally contains the eras side by side: **Festival of Britain/Southbank Centre (1951) → More London & The Scoop (2002) → One Blackfriars (2018)**, with **Elephant & Castle/Heygate** just inland. You can collect primary observation for the whole study in a few days on foot — ideal for ethics, cost and rigour. The river holds "waterfront regeneration" constant; only the *era/delivery model* varies.

### Failed-and-rebuilt pairs (your idea — same site, old vs new)
You wanted exemplars that *still exist*, plus some that **failed fast and were rebuilt**, so you can compare old vs new *on the same ground* and isolate regulation as the cause. Three verified Thames-side pairs (full identification logic in **`09`**):

| Pair | "Old" (failed) | "New" (rebuilt) |
|---|---|---|
| **P1** | **Robin Hood Gardens**, GLC Brutalist, 1972, Poplar/Blackwall; demolished 2017–2025 | **Blackwall Reach** (~1,575 homes, 45–60% affordable) |
| **P2** | **Heygate Estate**, GLC/Southwark, 1974, Elephant & Castle; demolished 2011–2014 | **Elephant Park** (Lendlease, market-led) |
| **P3** | **Thamesmead** stages 1–2, GLC riverside "town of the future", 1966–74; failed | **Peabody regeneration** (2014–) |

> **Critical:** comparing only old↔new does NOT control for technology or changing taste (the new build is also newer-tech and newer-fashion). To attribute the difference to *regulation*, you must also use **contemporaneous matched pairs** and **measure only regulation-sensitive attributes** — see `09 isolating regulation`. This is the dissertation's key methodological contribution.

## The debate your data will resolve (the heart of the dissertation)

| Pole | Claim | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Privatisation thesis** (pessimist) | Regeneration has *privatised* public space — POPS, surveillance, exclusion, "pseudo-public space". | Minton (2009/2012); The Guardian (Shenker, 2017); Németh & Schmidt (2011) |
| **Public-isation thesis** (revisionist) | Regeneration has *opened up* land (docks, industry, estates) that was **never genuinely public**; ownership matters less than experienced publicness. | Carmona (2017 blog; 2019; 2021) |

Carmona, 2017 (quotable): *"What we have witnessed is the 'public-isation' of private space."*
Minton, 2009 (quotable): the privatisation of streets "changing... the nature of public space, of citizenship and of trust."

**Your contribution:** neither side has *measured publicness consistently across time*. Your longitudinal scores test *which* thesis holds, and *when* — e.g. publicness may have fallen in Phase 1 (Docklands enclaves) but risen in Phase 4 (charter-era access). A nuanced, evidence-based verdict = Distinction.

## Why this still anchors to Ben Clifford
Clifford's thesis is that **deregulated, market-led delivery degrades the quality of outcomes** (his work: housing). You test the same thesis **over time, for public space**: does each step toward market-led delivery measurably reduce publicness? The viability mechanism (BPLN0041) supplies the "how". The Public London Charter tests whether *re-regulation* reverses it.
