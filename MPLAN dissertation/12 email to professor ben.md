The project has moved on considerably since we last spoke, and I think it has become both sharper and more clearly tied to your own work. A brief summary:

**1. Refined research question.** I have settled on a London-only, *longitudinal* design: *How has the publicness of public space produced through London's riverside regeneration changed across successive phases of urban policy from the post-war era to the present — and what explains the trajectory?* The comparison axis is time, not cities, organised into five policy eras (welfarist → property-led → urban renaissance → viability/austerity → Public London Charter era), read through the periodisation of neoliberalisation you suggested I "agree on".

**2. Literature and the debate I am adjudicating.** The work sits between Anna Minton's *privatisation* thesis (regeneration encloses and controls public space) and Matthew Carmona's revisionist *"public-isation"* thesis (regeneration opens up land that was never genuinely public). Neither side has measured publicness consistently across time, which is my gap. The frame comes from Peck & Tickell and Brenner et al. on variegated neoliberalisation and Harvey on entrepreneurial urbanism; the measurement from Németh & Schmidt's control index and Varna & Tiesdell's star model; and the mechanism from the viability literature (Ferm & Raco; Foye; McAllister et al.).

**3. Methodology — and the part I most want your view on.** It is **quantitative and observation-based, with no interviews**, which keeps the ethics light. The methodological core is an identification strategy that isolates *regulation* from *technology* and *taste*: I measure only regulation-sensitive attributes of publicness (ownership, secured access rights, posted rules, policing regime, S106-secured provision); I use same-site failed-and-rebuilt pairs (Robin Hood Gardens→Blackwall Reach; Heygate→Elephant Park; Thamesmead→Peabody) to hold location constant; I use contemporaneous matched pairs to hold technology and taste constant; and I map each shift to a datable regulatory discontinuity.

**4. Data — how it is gathered and interpreted.** Along a single walkable South Bank/Bankside transect (Festival of Britain/Southbank Centre → More London & The Scoop → One Blackfriars, with Elephant & Castle inland) I will score each exemplar with my own public-benefit delivery hierarchy plus the two published indices, add GIS metrics (accessible open space %, permeability, provision counts) and a 0–10 "regulatory regime index". The headline output is a scatter of regime index against publicness: if publicness falls as the regime becomes more market-led, and the points cluster by regime rather than by decade, that is visual quantitative evidence that regulation drives the change. With ~6–10 spaces I will treat this as intensive comparative, theory-testing work rather than statistical inference.

**5. Timetable.** I am consolidating the literature review and drafting the ethics form now, finalising the methodology and piloting the South Bank fieldwork next week, then drafting and integrating the proposal to submit on 29 June.

I see the project as a transposition of your own research programme: where your work shows that deregulated, market-led delivery degrades the *quality* of housing outcomes, I am testing whether the same holds for the *publicness* of public space — and doing so longitudinally, with the Public London Charter as a test of whether re-regulation reverses the trend.

A few specific things I would value your steer on:

4. **Archival sources** — for the historic and demolished exemplars, are there particular archives or collections you would point me to for original design intent and management rules?
